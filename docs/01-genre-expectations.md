# Lesson 1 — Genre Expectations (in plain English)

Readers pick books the way they pick cereal: by the shelf they are
standing in front of. Your cover's job is to say, silently and
instantly, "I belong on *this* shelf." This lesson tells you what each
major genre's shelf looks like.

These are conventions, not laws. But break one deliberately only after
you can explain exactly why — and only after your cover survives the
thumbnail test (Lesson 5).

## The quick-shelf guide

| Genre | Typical attitude | Typography | Imagery | Ick (avoid this) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Romance | Emotional promise; faces, dramatic light, soft texture | Elegant serif or hand-lettered script | Couple, embracing figures, flowers, sunsets, moody skies | Clip-art hearts, harsh neon-red text slabs |
| Thriller / Mystery | Ominous, fast, high contrast | Bold sans or slab, often outlined or distressed | Silhouettes, city at night, lone figure, red accent | Busy photos, tiny type, ten competing elements |
| Crime / Police procedural | Grit and paperwork — a poster with case-file energy | Stencil, condensed or distressed caps | Weapons, badges, crime tape, weathered surfaces | Comic bling, rainbow drop shadows |
| Fantasy | Epic scale, legend-rich, romantic | Ornate display serif, often logotype-style | Maps, runes, swords, dragons, big skies | Photos of people in Halloween costumes |
| Sci-Fi | Cool, engineered, future-clean | Geometric sans, comfortable with lowercase | Planets, ships, chrome, grids, neon | Glow effects hiding unreadable titles |
| Horror | Wrongness — one uncanny item on a plain background | Distressed serif; drips only if earned | A single object: doll hand, door ajar, shadow | Overkill — five scary things cancel out |
| Self-help / Business | Confident, generous, breathable | Big humanist sans, often one accent word | Simple shapes; apple, atom, or lightbulb metaphors | Stock-office poses, nine fonts |
| Cookbook | Delicious and warm, like a market stall | Bold friendly serif; the dish's name huge | Full-bleed food photo on a flat color | Cold, fluorescent-lit food photos |
| Memoir | Honest, quiet, person-first | Understated serif, restrained | A single portrait or one strong object | Suspense-poster tropes |
| History | Dignified, grounded, museum-poster | Sturdy serif for the title | Period painting, map, or manuscript | Retro clip-art parchment borders |
| Poetry | Whitespace is the point | Light serif or grotesque | One small object, lots of emptiness | Filling every corner |
| Kids' picture books | Friendly, saturated but not neon | Rounded, plump letterforms | Bubbly scenes, faces with visible eyes | Tiny dense paragraphs of text |

## The three genre moves everyone should steal

1. **One dominant image.** Not three visuals equal in weight. One. The
   others are supporting details at most.
2. **A type system of two fonts maximum.** One for the title (with
   personality), one for author and series (with discipline). Lesson 4.
3. **A palette of 2–3 colors plus one accent.** Choose them like a
   poster designer, not like a confetti fan.

## Fast genre self-test

Answer in one sentence each, out loud (yes, out loud):

1. If a stranger saw only my thumbnail for one second, what genre would
   they guess?
2. Which single element would I cut first if the cover felt crowded?
3. What is the *feeling* — haunted, confident, cozy? Does my title
   font actually look like it says that?

If you wrote "haunted" but your title font is a bouncy rounded sans,
Lesson 5 ("Fits the vibe?") is for you.
